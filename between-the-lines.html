At a glance
Problem
Journaling tools sit at two extremes: a blank page or a mood emoji. So more and more people take their inner life to a general chatbot. In a 2025 US survey, ~49% of people with an ongoing mental-health condition used one for support. The tool they confide in mostly agrees with them.
My role
Product & UX Designer, solo design and build. The journal started in the AI Product Masterclass (Nueve Design, 2026), and I kept building it after the course. It is now a working product: accounts, search by meaning, an AI Companion grounded in the journal, and an MCP server that lets an AI assistant read and write it with the owner’s consent.

01 · Origin

A course exercise became a product with a point of view

It started as a course project, suggested by a mentor as a way to learn a more technical toolchain. I steered it toward something I believed in and kept building after the course ended.

02 · The problem

People are already treating a chatbot as a therapist that only agrees with them

Therapy can feel expensive or hard to trust, so more people bring their mental health to a general-purpose chatbot. It knows nothing about their week. It tends to agree with them, and it can sound more certain than it should.

US survey of people with an ongoing mental health condition. Sentio University, 2025.

03 · The landscape

Most journaling apps are either an empty text box or a mood-emoji tracker

The blank page gives you nowhere to start. The mood tracker shrinks a whole day to one tapped face and never says anything back. Neither helps you understand what you wrote.

04 · Context

In a country that funds mental health heavily, it is still the costliest condition

Norway spends more on mental health than almost any country. It is still the single costliest condition there, close to three percent of GDP. Care exists. Access is uneven. The everyday layer, the one with low stakes, is thin. There is room before and beside professional care for something cheap and private. Something that helps a person notice their own patterns.

05 · Diagnosis

The first version worked, but it asked for too much at once

Writing, mood rating and tagging all sat on one screen. There was no focus while writing, and nothing came back for the effort.

06 · Guiding policy

One rule set the scope: support care, never replace it

The journal helps people notice their own patterns and never imitates a therapist. Each reflection carries the same line: patterns in your own words, not a diagnosis.

Built on established psychology

  • Russell’s circumplex model of affect, for mood
  • Pennebaker’s expressive-writing research, for the language reflection
  • CBT vocabulary, for the prompts
  • No low-validity personality quizzes, such as MBTI or the Enneagram
07 · The build

A focused two-step flow: write first, rate later

Writing and judging are different mental modes, so they became two steps. Step one is a distraction-free editor. Step two asks for mood and themes.

08 · Component

Mood became two axes, because calm and exhausted are not the same low

On a single sad-to-happy scale, calm and exhausted both score low, but they are very different days. So the emoji gave way to an Affect Grid, based on Russell’s circumplex: one axis for mood, one for energy, and one dot to capture both.

09 · Iteration

Dictation worked in Chrome and nowhere else, so it was rebuilt

The first version used the browser’s speech API, which failed silently outside Chrome. The rebuild records audio and transcribes it on the server with Whisper, so dictation works everywhere, including on phones.

10 · The payoff

It reads between the lines only when asked

The reward for journaling is the Between the Lines card. By default it compares this week with the last, next to a mood and energy trend, with no language model involved.

A deeper AI reflection is one deliberate tap away. Every entry does get a search embedding, so it can be found later by meaning.

11 · The Companion

The Companion is built the opposite way from a generic chatbot

It opens from your page and quotes what your week actually says. It asks a question back before it answers one. When a message in English or Norwegian signals a crisis, it stops the conversation and shows emergency numbers.

12 · How it works

The same system, explained to a ten-year-old, to a professional, and as it was built

AI products affect people who will never read the code, so being able to explain the system is part of building it. Each part below has three explanations. Choose one, or read all three.

Choose how it is explained
01

A private drawer for every person

Postgres · row-level security

Like I'm ten

Imagine a huge cupboard with a locked drawer for every person. When you open the app, the cupboard checks your key and opens only your drawer.

For professionals

Entries live in Postgres on Supabase, with row-level security on every user table. Server routes use a privileged role, so each one limits its queries to the signed-in user.

How I built it

The first version kept entries in the browser, the cheapest way to test whether writing felt right. Once it did, I moved them into a database with accounts.

02

Finding an entry by what it means

Embeddings · pgvector

Like I'm ten

Picture a giant map where every diary page is a dot. Pages about similar things sit close together, even in different words. "Worn out after work" lands next to "exhausted from the office". Your question becomes a dot too, and the app looks nearby.

For professionals

Each entry becomes one 1,536-dimension vector from OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small, stored in pgvector and compared by cosine similarity. Entries are short, so there is no chunking.

How I built it

Saving an entry must never fail because an AI service is down. So a database trigger only flags changed text, and a job running every two minutes computes the embeddings. If a run fails, the next one retries.

03

Searching in two ways at once

Hybrid search · trigrams · RRF

Like I'm ten

Two detectives look for the same page. One understands what you mean. The other checks the exact letters. Then they put their lists together, and a page that both of them found moves up.

For professionals

The top 30 entries by cosine similarity and the top 30 by trigram similarity are merged with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (k = 20). A search for "that argument" can find an entry that never uses the word.

How I built it

Most demo entries are in Polish, and the database has no Polish dictionary. Trigrams compare small groups of letters, so they work in any language and cope with word endings and typos.

04

Knowing when nothing matches

Similarity threshold · evaluation

Like I'm ten

If you ask for a page that does not exist, a bad helper brings you something anyway, and you believe it. So we drew a line: a page that is not similar enough stays on the shelf.

For professionals

Vector search always returns the nearest entries, even for nonsense. In the demo journal, gibberish scored up to 0.21 and real questions about 0.37 or higher, so the threshold went to 0.30.

How I built it

The first value, 0.15, would have handed 13% of the journal to the model for a nonsense query. I calibrated it once, with Claude Code, on a demo journal of 228 entries.

05

Looking things up before answering

RAG · context design

Like I'm ten

Before the Companion answers, a helper goes to the shelf and brings the pages that fit your question. It puts them on the desk, and the Companion answers from what is on the desk.

For professionals

Retrieval-augmented generation. For a question through the API or MCP, the server embeds it, runs hybrid search and builds the context from the focus day, the last seven days and up to 12 matching entries. If search fails, the Companion still answers from recent entries.

How I built it

A test question about something from seven months earlier got an answer from the last two weeks, because only those entries were in context. Adding search then pushed the current week out, so recent and relevant entries became two separate sets.

06

Keeping the AI in its place

System prompts · guardrails

Like I'm ten

The Companion carries a card with rules. Do not pretend to be a doctor. Only talk about what this person wrote. If someone sounds in danger, stop chatting and show who can really help.

For professionals

The system prompt forbids diagnoses and labels, and grounds replies in the person's entries. Prompts live on the server, so a browser cannot inject its own. A keyword check runs before the model is called.

How I built it

I kept the crisis check simple and independent of the model. When a message in English or Norwegian signals a crisis, the app skips the AI and shows emergency numbers in the conversation.

07

An engine that can be swapped

Model abstraction

Like I'm ten

The AI is like the engine in a car. Newer engines keep coming out, so the car is built to let you change the engine without rebuilding the whole car.

For professionals

Chat and embedding clients sit in one module: Grok for replies, OpenAI for embeddings. Changing the chat model is a one-line edit. Changing the embedding model means recomputing every stored vector.

How I built it

On the day the Companion launched, the provider retired the model I had chosen. Since then, each model name lives in one place.

08

A universal plug for AI assistants

Model Context Protocol (MCP)

Like I'm ten

MCP is a universal plug for AI assistants. The journal has one socket and a list of what it can do, so an assistant like Claude can plug in and say "write this down" or "find that page".

For professionals

A stateless MCP server over Streamable HTTP exposes six tools, such as create_entry, search_entries and ask_companion. Each has a typed schema, and the HTTP API and MCP share the same functions and rules.

How I built it

Tool descriptions turned out to be design work, with a model as the reader. The search tool tells it how to read the scores and that weak matches can come back. I use this connection myself: Claude can add an entry to my journal and read it back.

09

A guest pass, never your password

OAuth 2.1 · PKCE

Like I'm ten

You would not give a stranger your house keys, so you give the assistant a guest pass. It works for one hour. If someone steals an old pass and tries to use it, the whole set stops working.

For professionals

The journal is its own OAuth 2.1 authorisation server, with dynamic client registration, a consent screen and PKCE. Access tokens last one hour, refresh tokens rotate on every use, and replaying a used one revokes the chain.

How I built it

Claude's connectors do not accept a pasted API key, so connecting the journal needed a real authorisation flow. I specified the behaviour and the threat, a stolen token, and Claude Code wrote the implementation.

Who did what

I wrote the requirements, worked out the architecture with Claude Code, made the product and safety decisions, and checked the results in the running product. Claude Code wrote most of the code. I am a designer who builds, and I can explain every part above. The journal has no real users yet.

Not a mockup to imagine, a product to open.

13 · Accessibility

The component that defines this product is the one most likely to lock someone out

A square you drag a dot into only works with a pointer, and it is the heart of this product. So it also works as a slider: focusable, driven by the arrow keys, and announcing its position in words, such as “pleasant, low energy”.

The Companion had a quieter bug: its typing indicator said nothing to a screen reader, so the wait was pure silence. It became a live region.

An automated scan catches the contrast. It does not catch a grid that only answers to a mouse.

14 · Lessons

What the project taught, and where it goes next

  • Removing things helped more than adding them. The navigation, the clutter and the single mood scale all came out.
  • People can trust an AI feature that is on demand, clearly labelled and never diagnoses.
  • With no real users yet, every choice is still a hypothesis. Testing with people who keep a journal comes next.
