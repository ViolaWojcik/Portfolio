Paths, not plans

A hospital in a protected building believed it had run out of room.
So we followed the paths of patients and staff through it, and let those paths show what to change first.

RoleArchitect · Service designer
TeamArchitects · HR · Finance
ContextHistoric hospital with an oncology ward (anonymised)
Year2016
ScopePatient experience, patient pathways, staff flows, space use, zoning
MethodsObservation, patient and clinician interviews, pathway mapping, stakeholder engagement, co-creation with clinicians
At a glance
Problem
A hospital in a protected 19th-century building believed it had run out of room. Patients on the national cancer fast-track had a coordinator and came to the clinic three times before treatment. Patients with a standard referral came at least five times and could wait three to four months for one appointment.
My role
Architect, working as a service designer, on a value-based hospital audit at Vita Management, with HR and finance specialists. I analysed how patients and staff move through the building: three months of observation, many interviews with patients, physicians and nurses, patient pathways worked out with clinicians, and a room-by-room analysis of space.
Value
  • Funded: the hospital used the audit in its application and received renovation funding
  • Built almost at once: a laundry beside chemotherapy became registration and consulting rooms, ending the walk between buildings before each session
  • One plan for the whole hospital: recommendations for outpatients, inpatients, staff and visitors, built around three access zones
Real client work · anonymised · figures from our 2016 audit
01 · The patient

Two patients came to the same clinic, and only one had someone organising their care

We mapped each patient's route from the GP's referral to the start of treatment. It depended on one thing: whether they held the national fast-track card for suspected cancer.

With the card, a coordinator arranged the appointments and tests, collected the results and told the patient what came next. The first appointment came after about five days. The patient came to the clinic three times before treatment, plus any lab visits.

Without the card, patients organised everything themselves. They waited three to four months for an appointment, took a referral to each lab, waited two to three weeks for results, then booked again, often months ahead. That meant at least five visits.

The fast-track also guaranteed shorter waits, so the coordinator was only part of the difference. But the comparison showed how much organising work the hospital was leaving to its patients.

02 · Fieldwork

We spent three months watching how the hospital really worked

The hospital commissioned an audit of its finances, staffing and clinical work. My part was the clinical space: how the building worked for patients and staff.

I observed clinics, wards, labs and waiting areas at different times, and carried out many interviews myself with patients, including palliative patients, physicians and nurses.

We mapped movement for each group separately: patients by treatment, doctors by specialism, nurses between station and bed, and ambulances on their way to Admissions. Then we worked out the patient pathways together with physicians and nurses.

03 · The people

Patients and staff had each found ways to cope with what the hospital lacked

Many patients ate their meals on their beds, because their rooms had no table and the canteen was for staff only. Wards had few places to sit with family, and many toilets were kept locked.

The hospital had no proper staff rooms, so nurses made rest corners inside the duty room, behind a partition. In one room this took more than a third of the floor and cut the daylight at the desks.

These workarounds went into the audit as findings: needs the building had never met.

04 · The analysis

The hospital said it had no room, so we measured every floor

We colour-coded every room on all five floors by use. The rooms where patients slept, washed and spent the day took 14% of the complex.

Some rooms held six beds, where national rules allow five, and many beds could not be reached from three sides, as the same rules require.

The space could come from inside the building. Divided wards each kept their own nurses' station, treatment room and staff area. Merging some of them would free rooms for patients and allow fewer beds per room.

05 · Findings

We grouped every finding by the people it affected

  • Outpatients: no central registration, clinics on two floors and a separate desk for almost every lab
  • Inpatients: too many beds per room, too few toilets, no day rooms and nowhere to eat
  • Staff: no proper staff rooms, and nurses' stations far from some beds and out of sight of the ward entrance
  • Everyone: no clear zones for the public, patients and staff, and no plans or signs to find the way
  • The building: a protected monument, with ward doors too narrow for a bed and walls that could not move without the conservator's approval
06 · The stakes

For patients near the end of life, a few metres could decide how they spent the day

One ward cared for many palliative patients. I talked with them about their week, their room and the distance to the bathroom. Sometimes, by my next visit, that person was gone.

For someone that ill, the distance to a toilet, or a chair for a relative beside the bed, shapes the whole day. Those needs went into the audit alongside the floor areas.

07 · The decisions

Five decisions turned the findings into a plan the hospital could act on

  1. Organise by who uses the space. Fixing problems one by one would have left the cause in place, so the plan created access zones for outpatients, inpatients and visitors.
  2. Work with the original plan. The protected walls could not move, so wards stayed in the wings, surgery and treatment formed a core, and admissions, clinics and diagnostics shared one public area.
  3. Bring outpatient care together. All clinics on one floor, with one central registration that also served diagnostics.
  4. Leave the hospital a real choice. Admissions could be reorganised where it stood or moved to where ambulances could manoeuvre more easily. We drew both options.
  5. Prioritise the change that asks least of the building. Before each chemotherapy session, patients walked to another building for tests and back. A large room beside chemotherapy held the laundry, which could move to an outside provider.

With partition walls alone, that room became registration and consulting rooms next to chemotherapy. The hospital built it almost at once, and the walk between buildings ended.

The hospital then used the audit to apply for renovation funding, and the funding was granted.

08 · Reflection

What I would do differently today, and where AI could help

I interviewed patients, but we worked out the pathways with physicians and nurses. Today, patients and families would help design them too, and I would measure the effect: visits before treatment, days to the first appointment, and what patients and staff report.

I would start any AI work from the coordinator. An AI assistant could tell patients in plain language where to go, what to bring and what happens next, and show the coordinator which results are still missing. Test results, prognosis, treatment decisions and how a patient feels stay with a named nurse or doctor. I explore the same questions in Between the Lines.

Patients with a coordinator had one person who knew what came next.
